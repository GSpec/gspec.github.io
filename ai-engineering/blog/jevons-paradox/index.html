Jevons’ 19th-Century Paradox Predicts AI’s Impact on Jobs

As engineers, we are conditioned to view efficiency as the ultimate virtue. If a system becomes more efficient, we conserve resources. We deploy this logic to optimization problems every day.

We are currently applying this same engineering logic to the adoption of Generative AI. The argument seems straightforward:

AI can automate task X, making the output of task X significantly more efficient. Therefore, the business will require fewer resources to achieve the original output.

For many business leaders and anxious employees, this translates to an inevitable conclusion: efficiency gains will lead to massive job displacement as demand for human capital shrinks.

However, if we look through the lens of economic history, specifically that of the 19th-century economist William Stanley Jevons, this conclusion may be fundamentally flawed. AI is likely to dramatically alter the definition of “business as usual,” increasing rather than decreasing the total demand for specific human skills. This phenomenon is known as the Jevons Paradox.

Who was William Stanley Jevons?

William Stanley Jevons was a seminal English economist and logician, born in Liverpool in 1835. He is recognized as one of the key figures in the marginalist revolution, which shifted economic focus to utility and individual choice.

In 1865, he published his most influential work, “The Coal Question”. Britain was the industrial heart of the world, fueled entirely by coal. Policymakers and industrialists were increasingly worried that Britain would eventually run out of coal, crippling the industrial economy.

At the time, the solution seemed obvious: invest in and invent more efficient steam engines that would consume less coal per unit of work. They believed that better efficiency would solve the resource constraint.

Jevons made a brilliant, counter-intuitive observation. He looked at the historical data following James Watt’s significant improvements to the steam engine (which dramatically increased its efficiency). He observed that the increased efficiency did not lead to a reduction in total coal consumption. In fact, total coal consumption exploded.

Jevons concluded that as technology increases the efficiency with which a resource is used, the total consumption of that resource increases rather than decreases.

Understanding the Jevons Paradox

The Jevons Paradox works on a simple principle of elasticity and opportunity cost:

  1. Increased Efficiency: Technology allows a unit of output (say, a mile traveled or a kilowatt generated) to require fewer resources (coal, oil, etc.).
  2. Reduced Unit Cost: Because the output is more efficient, the cost per unit of that output drops dramatically.
  3. Increased Demand: When a resource becomes significantly cheaper, the demand for it becomes “elastic.” People and businesses find entire new uses for that resource that were previously uneconomical. The marginal utility of using the resource remains high.

Increased efficiency lowers the cost barriers to utilization, and society responds by exploiting that efficiency gain to do much more of the original activity (and inventing new activities) rather than simply banking the savings.

Applying Jevons to AI

If we replace “coal” with “intelligent human task-time” and “steam engines” with “Generative AI agents,” we can see the Jevons Paradox repeating itself.

Currently, we are obsessed with the efficiency gains: AI allows a “unit of cognitive task completion” (writing an email, reviewing code, summarizing documentation) to be completed more efficiently.

The mainstream narrative predicts that this efficiency gain will lead directly to job losses as businesses “bank” the savings. Jevons predicts the opposite.

AI is likely to make cognitive work so cheap and accessible that the definition of what constitutes necessary output will shift radically. The elasticity of demand for complex, non-automatable cognitive work will spike.

Business Costs and Total Capital Demand

In the short term, businesses adopting AI will see unit-level cost reductions. A marketing department can generate 10 times the content for the same cost.

In the long term, competitive pressure will ensure that “10x content” becomes the new baseline. To remain competitive, every business will be required to exploit these efficiency gains. Businesses will shift from banking the savings to reinvesting them to achieve massive increases in scale, complexity, or personalization.

Jevons suggests that the total capital spent on the overall activity of human-augmented cognitive tasks will likely increase, as the total volume and value derived from that activity expand.

The Effect on Jobs

The Jevons Paradox suggests that while the demand for basic task repetition will collapse (which is the source of anxiety), the total demand for human resources is likely to increase, specifically for resources that can execute complex problem definition, systems integration, strategic reasoning, and nuanced creativity.

The effect on the workforce will be split across short-term disruption and long-term adaptation.

Short-Term Workforce Disruption

In the short term, the Jevons Paradox causes significant instability.

The initial shock of AI automation can displace workers whose entire job description is limited to the tasks AI can automate efficiently (e.g. standard data entry, boilerplate copy generation, basic code updates, language translations, etc.).

The bottleneck in the short term is not the demand for the output, but the speed at which businesses can adapt their infrastructure to handle the increased volume of work that AI now makes possible. While the total work defined by Jevons will eventually materialize, specific individuals whose skills are highly narrow face genuine risks in the transition period.

Long-Term Workforce Adaptation and Job Availability

In the long term, Jevons wins.

As the unit cost of basic cognitive tasks drops, entire industries that were previously uneconomical will become viable. Entirely new classes of jobs will emerge to define, integrate, manage, and audit the now-massive flows of AI-generated output.

For example, when a software engineer can generate code at 5 times the speed, the problem shifts from code volume to code quality at scale, system integration and validation. We will need more engineers to manage and govern this vastly larger and more complex code base, even if they aren’t spending as much time writing basic functions from scratch.

The marginal utility of human resource deployment moves further up the complexity ladder.

Conclusion: Engineering for the Jevons Paradox

As engineers adopting AI, we should remember the lesson of Jevons. We must not measure the ultimate impact of this technology solely by the efficiency gain of a single, defined task.

We must recognize that efficiency is not a destination, but a springboard. The adoption of AI is not an optimization problem with a static definition of “finished work”. It is the mechanism by which the entire system of human knowledge work is being radically redefined.

The real challenge is not managing the cost savings; it is designing and governing the inevitable, explosive increase in complexity and scale that those savings will unlock. In an AI-driven world, the demand for high-order, non-automatable human capital remains the ultimate scarce resource.
